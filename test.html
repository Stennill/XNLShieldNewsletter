THIS WEEK'S THREAT

"The Fake Toll Road Text Scam"

Also called: EZPass scam, toll violation text, unpaid toll message

📖 How This Scam Works

It starts with a text message that looks official. You get a text that appears to be from your state's toll road system — maybe EZPass, SunPass, FasTrak, or another familiar name. The message says you have an unpaid toll, usually a small amount like $4.35 or $6.99. It warns that if you don't pay immediately, you'll face a late fee of $50 or more, or even have your license suspended.

The message includes a link. They want you to click on it to "pay your balance" right away. The link looks almost real — something like "ezpass-payment.com" or "tollservices-alert.com" — but it's a fake website created by scammers.

If you click and enter your information, they steal it. The fake website asks for your credit card number, your driver's license, and sometimes your Social Security number. The scammers then use this information to steal your money, open accounts in your name, or sell your details to other criminals.

Why it's so effective: The small dollar amount makes people think "I'll just pay it and be done with it." The threat of fees or license suspension creates panic. And because many of us DO use toll roads, the message seems believable. Scammers are sending millions of these texts — even to people who don't drive or don't have toll accounts!

🔍 How To Spot This Scam

✓ The phone number looks weird. Real toll agencies don't text from random 10-digit numbers or numbers starting with +1. Look for numbers like "234-555-8901" — that's suspicious.

✓ The website link doesn't match the real one. Your actual toll agency's website ends in .gov or a well-known .com (like ezpassnj.com). Scam links have extra words or hyphens like "ezpass-service-alert.com" or "toll-payment-center.net."

✓ They create urgency. Phrases like "IMMEDIATE ACTION REQUIRED," "Pay within 24 hours," or "Avoid suspension" are red flags. Real agencies send paper mail first and give you weeks to respond.

✓ They ask for too much information. A toll payment should only need basic payment info — NOT your Social Security number, driver's license photo, or bank login details.

✓ You don't even have a toll account! Many people receiving these texts don't have EZPass or haven't driven on toll roads. The scammers send these messages randomly, hoping to catch someone who does.

✅ What To Do If You Get One

1

DO NOT click the link. Not even to "see what happens." Just leave it alone.

2

If you're worried it might be real, go directly to your toll agency's website by typing it into your browser yourself (or call the number on your actual toll statement). NEVER use the link in the text.

3

Report it. Forward the text to 7726 (that spells "SPAM"). This helps your phone company block these scammers. You can also report it at reportfraud.ftc.gov

4

Delete the message. Once you've reported it, get rid of it so you're not tempted to click later.

⚠️ Already clicked and entered information?

Don't panic, but act fast. Call your bank or credit card company RIGHT NOW and tell them your card may be compromised. They can freeze it and send you a new one. If you entered your Social Security number, consider placing a free fraud alert at annualcreditreport.com.

⚡ QUICK WIN: Your 30-Second Safety Boost

Right now, take 30 seconds to save your toll agency's real phone number in your contacts.

That way, if you ever get a suspicious text, you can call the real number instantly to check if it's legitimate. Search "[your state] toll road customer service" — the real number will be on a .gov website. Save it as "REAL EZPass" or "REAL Toll Road" so future-you knows it's trustworthy!

The FBI reported a 900% increase in toll scam complaints since 2024. You're not alone in seeing these — and now you know exactly what to do!
